Customers — who actually buys this

Online retailers — I'd skip them. Cost-driven, hyperscaler-native, sovereignty is not in their buying criteria unless they sell to government. Scope — what to offer and, more importantly, what not to
Your instinct is right: data first, then compute, then stop. Here's how I'd structure the offering:
Lead with: sovereignty assessment + migration of data and compute. That's the wedge. Concretely:

Sovereignty Assessment (2–4 weeks, €15–30k). Inventory of US cloud dependencies, classification by CLOUD Act exposure / Schrems II risk / regulatory relevance (NIS2, DORA, AVG), migration roadmap with cost and complexity matrix per workload. This is your lead magnet. Cheap, high-margin, generates the bigger deal.
Data migration. Object storage, databases, data warehouses, backups. Land on Dutch/EU providers — StackIT, OVHcloud, Scaleway, Leaseweb, Fuga, TransIP, Intermax, depending on workload fit.
Compute migration. VMs and Kubernetes lift-and-shift, plus selective refactoring where the source uses proprietary AWS/Azure services (Lambda, Cosmos, etc.).
Managed sovereign cloud operations. This is the recurring revenue. After migration, you run it for them. Patching, monitoring, optimisation, compliance reporting. This is what makes the LTV work.

Offer if asked, but don't lead with:

Identity (IdP). Entra ID is deeply embedded; a full rip-and-replace is months of pain. The right answer is usually federation: keep Entra for SSO, move sensitive workloads behind an EU IdP (Keycloak, or a managed Dutch offering), broker between them. Sell it as a hardening project, not a migration.
PaaS components without hyperscaler equivalents — message queues, certain managed databases, search. You'll have to do these because real apps need them, but scope them per-project, don't sell them as a product line.

Don't offer at all (or refer to partners):

Office suite migration (M365 → Nextcloud/OnlyOffice/Collabora). Massive change management burden, productivity hit, user revolt risk. The Linux + MijnBureau pilots in 's-Hertogenbosch, Zaanstad, Ede and Amsterdam will run through end of 2026 — let that pilot mature and pick up follow-on work in 2027+ if the model proves out. For now, partner with someone like Nextcloud directly if a customer insists. Ictmagazine
Email migration off Exchange/Gmail. Deliverability and address-book pain. Refer out.
ERP/CRM/SAP. Not your business. SAP is German but customers run it on hyperscalers; the migration is an SAP-consultancy job, not a sovereignty job.
Endpoint/MDM, network/SD-WAN. Adjacent but a different muscle.
Application development. If your parent company already does this, fine, route it there. But don't position your new unit as a dev shop — it dilutes the message.
Building your own datacenter or cloud. You're a migration and ops specialist, not an infrastructure provider. The capex is wrong for what you're trying to do, and you'd compete with your own partners.

The discipline here is important. The incumbents — Centric, Schuberg Philis, KPN, the big four consultancies — will sell customers a full-stack story. Your edge is being focused, faster, and not trying to lock the customer into your own platform.

The stake — the structural fork comes first
Before any percentage conversation, there's a binary that determines everything: a real ownership stake requires a separate legal entity. You can't own a slice of one business unit inside Cinq BV. If sovereignty stays a unit inside Cinq, the most you can get is synthetic — phantom equity, profit-share, or SARs (stock appreciation rights). Those are contractual rights to economic upside, not ownership. If you genuinely want to own part of this, the ask has to be a new werkmaatschappij — call it whatever, "Cinq Sovereign" or a standalone brand — in which you hold founder shares, with Cinq/Innovatech as majority shareholder providing capital, people and name.
In the Dutch context you'd typically hold those shares through your own persoonlijke holding BV rather than personally. The reason is the deelnemingsvrijstelling: if your holding owns 5% or more of the new entity, dividends and any eventual exit proceeds flow up to your holding without corporate tax, deferred until you take money out personally. The 5% line also triggers DGA status (gebruikelijkloon rules) and box-2 treatment. I'm not a tax advisor and the rates and thresholds shift, so this is a conversation to have with a fiscalist before you sign anything — but knowing the menu lets you ask for the right structure rather than accepting whatever's offered.
Now the honest part about how much. You told me you can't take on all the risk and all the work, and that's completely reasonable — but it sits in direct tension with a large stake. Ownership tracks what you put at risk: capital, foregone salary, or sole accountability. Cinq is bringing the balance sheet, ~50 engineers, the brand, the client network and the back office; you're bringing the idea, the StackIT relationship, the thesis and your drive. The idea and one partner relationship alone don't command much. What justifies a meaningful stake is your willingness to own and drive it over years. So realistically, for an intrapreneur in your position, something in the 10–25% range of the new entity, held via your holding, vesting over three to four years, is a credible ask. If you want the top of that range or beyond, you trade for it — put in some capital, or take a reduced base salary in exchange for more equity. Decide where on that risk/reward curve you actually want to sit; that's your single most important pre-pitch decision.
The one negotiation move that matters most: lock in the equity framework now, before you build it, while your leverage is highest. Your leverage is the idea plus your willingness — both of which evaporate the moment you've proven the model or shown you'll do it regardless. Get a written agreement in principle (an intentieovereenkomst or term sheet) up front, with the shares vesting against time and milestones. Don't build first and negotiate equity after; you'll be negotiating from zero.

Refer that out or scope it as a separate program; don't let it contaminate your clean data-and-compute pitch.
On where to land it, the alternatives are real but I'd be honest with clients that they're early. Office.eu launched in March 2026 from The Hague, Nextcloud-based and 100% European-owned, and Euro-Office, a fork of OnlyOffice from an IONOS/Nextcloud-led consortium, hits stable release on June 9 — that's four days from now. It's already tangled in legal disputes with OnlyOffice's maintainers and a fight over document-format standards. The building blocks underneath — Nextcloud with OnlyOffice or Collabora — are mature enough that the ICC moved off Microsoft 365 to a European open-source suite in November 2025, but I wouldn't stake a client's daily operations on a productivity suite that GA'd last week. "Real and improving fast, not yet safe for your whole workforce" is the honest line, and it fits your register. Yeandel + 3
The reusable lesson for when you review the framework: decompose every sticky Microsoft product into its data layer and its platform layer, and score them separately. SharePoint, OneDrive, even Exchange all split the same way. That decomposition is what keeps you in the achievable-data-migration lane instead of accidentally selling an office-suite revolution.

# Sovereignty Assessment — Framework & Delivery Playbook

*Draft v0.1 — productized wedge offering for the sovereignty practice*

---

## 1. What this is (and what it is not)

The Sovereignty Assessment is a fixed-scope, fixed-price engagement that answers one
question for a client: **which of our data and workloads are exposed to foreign
jurisdiction, how bad is that exposure, and what would it take to fix it?**

It produces a prioritized, costed roadmap — not a migration, not a legal opinion, not
an infrastructure build. It is the wedge: low commitment for the client, high signal for
us, and it sets up the migration engagement that follows.

**Positioning discipline.** Sell control and jurisdiction, not "cheaper" or "faster."
The honest story — and the one the benchmark data supports — is that European providers
are predictable and good-enough, occasionally pricier, and that the value is in who can
be compelled to hand over data, who can switch you off, and whether you can leave. Never
promise zero foreign exposure; that promise is not credible and clients know it.

**Explicitly out of scope** (mention only if asked, scope separately): office-suite
migration, email migration, full IdP replacement, ERP/CRM/SAP, the migration execution
itself, and building any infrastructure.

---

## 2. Two tiers — the funnel

| | **Quick Scan** | **Full Assessment** |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | ~1 week | 3–4 weeks |
| Indicative price | €7–10k | €15–30k |
| Output | Dependency inventory + exposure heatmap | Everything in Quick Scan + feasibility scoring, prioritized roadmap, target landing zones, cost projection, compliance mapping |
| Purpose | Cheap front door; create urgency with a picture of reality | The fundable plan; hands off directly to a migration SOW |
| Sells | The Full Assessment | The migration engagement |

The Quick Scan exists because most clients genuinely **do not know where their data
runs** — that knowledge gap is the most common entry point. Show them the map, and the
Full Assessment sells itself.

---

## 3. The analytical core — the classification model

Every in-scope workload, dataset, or service is scored on two axes. The intersection
drives the roadmap.

### Axis A — Sovereignty Exposure ("how much should we worry")

Score each 1–5, weighted, roll up to a single exposure rating.

1. **Data residency & jurisdiction** — Is the provider US-owned or otherwise reachable
   by foreign legal compulsion (CLOUD Act and equivalents)? Note: a hyperscaler
   "sovereign cloud" with EU staff but US parent does **not** clear this lens — the
   Solvinity case turned on ownership, not where the engineers sit.
2. **Regulatory trigger** — Does NIS2 (NL: Cyberbeveiligingswet), DORA, AVG/GDPR
   (incl. Schrems II transfer rules), or BIO impose specific obligations on this
   workload? Higher when the workload is in scope of a hard regime.
3. **Data sensitivity & criticality** — Special-category personal data, citizen data,
   vital-process data score high. Public/non-personal data scores low.
4. **Concentration risk** — How much does this depend on a single provider or a single
   provider's proprietary services? Single-vendor lock-in raises exposure.
5. **Who holds the keys** — Encryption key custody, admin/root access, operational
   control. If the provider can technically read or freeze it, exposure is higher.

### Axis B — Migration Feasibility ("how hard to move")

Score each 1–5, roll up to an effort rating.

1. **Architecture portability** — Containerized + infrastructure-as-code (Kubernetes +
   Terraform) is low effort; proprietary PaaS (Lambda, Cosmos DB, managed
   hyperscaler-only services) is high effort. *This is where the benchmark thesis pays
   off: the K8s/Terraform layer is the portability backbone.*
2. **Data gravity** — Volume, egress cost, and how tightly coupled the data is to other
   systems.
3. **Functional parity** — Does a credible EU provider offer the equivalent service? If
   there's no equivalent (some GPU, some niche PaaS), effort/uncertainty is high.
4. **Integration dependencies** — Ties to IdP/SSO, managed databases, message queues,
   third-party SaaS.
5. **Operational readiness** — Does the client's team have the skills to run the target,
   or do they need managed operations afterward (a phase-two upsell)?

### The matrix

```
                 LOW migration effort        HIGH migration effort
              +---------------------------+---------------------------+
HIGH exposure |   QUICK WINS              |   STRATEGIC MOVES         |
              |   Do first. Fund now.     |   Plan, phase, budget.    |
              |   These prove value fast. |   These are the big SOWs. |
              +---------------------------+---------------------------+
LOW exposure  |   OPPORTUNISTIC           |   LEAVE ALONE             |
              |   Move if convenient,     |   Explicitly recommend    |
              |   e.g. bundled with a     |   NOT moving. This honesty |
              |   nearby quick win.       |   earns trust.            |
              +---------------------------+---------------------------+
```

## 4. Delivery process (Full Assessment, 3–4 weeks)

**Phase 0 — Kickoff (days 1–2).** Confirm scope and boundaries, identify stakeholders
(CISO/CIO, architects, data owners, procurement), secure read access to cloud accounts
and architecture docs. Agree the workload list to be assessed.

**Phase 1 — Discovery & inventory (week 1).** Stakeholder interviews using the standard
guide + technical discovery (cloud config/billing scan, architecture review) to build
the dependency inventory. Goal: a complete map of what runs where, on whose
infrastructure, under whose jurisdiction.

**Phase 2 — Classification & analysis (week 2).** Score every item on both axes using
the rubric. Place on the matrix. Map regulatory triggers. Identify concentration risks.

**Phase 3 — Roadmap & target architecture (week 3).** For the Quick Wins and Strategic
Moves, propose target landing zones from the reference patterns (section 6). Build the
phased roadmap, honest cost projection, and compliance mapping.

**Phase 4 — Readout (week 4).** Two artifacts, two audiences: an executive summary for
the board (risk, options, cost, recommendation) and a technical appendix for the
engineers (per-workload scoring, target architectures, migration notes). Live
presentation, then handoff to a proposed migration SOW.

---

## 5. Deliverables

- **Dependency inventory** — the map of what runs where and under whose jurisdiction.
- **Exposure heatmap** — visual, board-ready, the "oh no" artifact.
- **Prioritization matrix** — the four quadrants, every workload placed.
- **Target landing zones** — recommended EU provider + architecture per movable workload.
- **Cost projection** — honest, including cases where sovereign costs more.
- **Compliance mapping** — which workloads satisfy / risk NIS2, DORA, AVG, BIO.
- **Executive summary + technical appendix** — board and engineers, respectively.
- **Proposed migration roadmap / SOW** — the bridge to the next engagement.

---

## 6. Reference landing zones (what makes it repeatable)

Don't redesign per client. Maintain a small library of vetted target patterns:

- **Containerized web/app workload** → managed Kubernetes (StackIT or OVHcloud) +
  managed PostgreSQL. The default, benchmark-backed path.
- **Object storage / data lake** → S3-compatible EU storage (OVH, Scaleway).
- **Classic VMs** → EU IaaS (Leaseweb, Fuga, OVH), lift-and-shift.
- **Cost-sensitive, hands-on team** → Hetzner IaaS with self-managed k3s (flag the
  operational trade-off explicitly).
- **Managed databases / queues** → provider-native EU equivalents, per-workload check.

**Known gaps to declare up front** (this is part of the honesty):
- GPU / large-scale AI compute — limited EU options, often a "leave alone for now."
- Certain proprietary PaaS — no clean equivalent; refactor or defer.
- Identity — recommend federation (keep Entra, broker sensitive workloads to an EU IdP)
  rather than rip-and-replace.

---

## 7. The repeatable toolkit (manageability)

The whole point is that a small team runs this without reinventing it each time:

- **Interview guide** — standard stakeholder questions per role.
- **Discovery toolkit** — scripts to pull cloud config/billing into the inventory
  (a natural extension of the existing benchmark tooling: a "dependency scanner").
- **Scoring rubric** — the 1–5 definitions for all ten factors, so scoring is consistent
  across consultants and clients.
- **Report templates** — exec summary + technical appendix, pre-structured.
- **Reference pattern library** — section 6, maintained and versioned.

Build these once; each subsequent assessment gets faster and more profitable.

---

## 8. Land-and-expand

The roadmap *is* the migration sales document. Sequence:

1. **Quick Scan** → reveals reality, creates urgency → sells the Full Assessment.
2. **Full Assessment** → produces the funded roadmap → sells the first migration
   (start with Quick Wins; they prove value fast and de-risk the relationship).
3. **Migration** → on completion, the client now runs a sovereign environment they may
   not have the team to operate → optional **managed operations** (phase-two, recurring
   revenue — but a different operating model for Cinq, decide deliberately).

---

## 9. Positioning guardrails (keep these visible)

- Lead with jurisdiction and control, never "cheaper/faster."
- Never promise zero foreign exposure; manage the dependency, don't pretend it away.
- Be proud of the "leave alone" quadrant — restraint is a credibility signal.
- Regulatory mapping flags obligations; it is not legal advice. Recommend the client's
  own legal/compliance review for anything load-bearing.
- Cost realism up front. A surprise on price later kills trust and the migration deal.
